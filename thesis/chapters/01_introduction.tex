\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Motivation}

Customer-feedback analytics requires more than a document-level positive or
negative judgement. A single review can praise the attitude of staff while
criticising an application, or approve of a discount while judging the ordinary
price unfavourably. Aspect-Based Sentiment Analysis (ABSA) addresses this
fine-grained setting by associating sentiment with a particular aspect rather
than assigning one polarity to the whole document
\cite{pontiki2016semeval,zhang2023survey}. In an operational system, however,
the aspect taxonomy is rarely immutable. Organisations introduce new products,
channels, policies, and reporting categories, while supervised classifiers are
normally trained for a fixed output space.

This dissertation studies that mismatch. The task is not unrestricted topic
discovery: a model is not asked to invent or name arbitrary categories.
Instead, it receives a supplied set of canonical candidate aspects and predicts
the applicable aspect--sentiment pairs. Each candidate is judged separately, so
the output can contain several aspects with different sentiments or be empty.
This candidate-label formulation keeps evaluation exact while representing a
realistic taxonomy-maintenance problem.

\section{Research Gap}

Zero-shot text classification has considered labels absent from task-specific
training, including the label-partially-unseen setting in which a model is
trained on some labels and evaluated jointly on seen and unseen labels
\cite{yin2019benchmarking}. Multi-label work has additionally used label
descriptions and known label structures to transfer to rare or unseen labels
\cite{rios2018structured,liu2021hierarchy}. Within ABSA, natural-language
prompts, auxiliary sentences, and weak label-side supervision show that an
aspect can be supplied as semantic input rather than represented only by a
fixed classifier index
\cite{sun2019auxiliary,seoh2021open,huang2020weakly}.

These strands do not by themselves answer how an aspect-sentiment system
degrades as the taxonomy shift becomes progressively harder. A singleton unseen
candidate does not compete with familiar labels; a jointly evaluated unseen
candidate does. Two unseen aspects introduce competition between unfamiliar
labels, while withholding a complete parent group removes an entire supervised
sub-taxonomy. The reviewed literature also does not provide a directly matched
ABSA protocol for testing asymmetric documentation, where two unseen aspects
are predicted together but only one receives a natural-language definition.
The dissertation therefore treats the evaluation design, rather than a flat
model leaderboard, as its organising contribution.

\section{Research Questions}

The study is organised around four questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RQ1: Taxonomy difficulty.} How quickly do lexical, frozen
    semantic, supervised contextual, and language-model methods degrade as the
    task moves from a supplied singleton unseen aspect to mixed seen/unseen
    candidate sets, two unseen aspects, and unseen parent groups?

    \item \textbf{RQ2: Label semantics.} How much unseen-aspect performance is
    recovered by adding one frozen minimal taxonomy definition to the canonical
    aspect name, and does asymmetric description availability bias prediction
    towards the described candidate?

    \item \textbf{RQ3: Task adaptation.} Under a matched candidate-pair
    interface, does QLoRA adaptation improve on a frozen Qwen control, and is
    any gain achieved without sacrificing performance on unseen labels or
    destabilising seen-label predictions?

    \item \textbf{RQ4: Reliability.} Are the main differences stable under
    paired review-level resampling and across the registered aspect or
    parent-group units, and what precision, recall, calibration, and runtime
    trade-offs accompany them?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Controlled Difficulty Ladder}

The active experiment route contains four levels. Level~1 is
supplied-candidate single-unseen leave-one-aspect-out (LOAO): one aspect is
removed from task-specific training and is the only candidate judged at
evaluation. Level~2 retains the same twelve outer folds but evaluates all twelve
aspects jointly, so one unseen label competes with eleven seen labels. This is
the dissertation's closest analogue to label-partially-unseen or generalised
zero-shot text classification.

Level~3 removes two aspects at a time and evaluates all twelve candidates. Its
core \texttt{NN}/\texttt{DN}/\texttt{ND}/\texttt{DD} crossover independently
varies whether the two unseen aspects receive their frozen minimal definitions;
the ten seen aspects retain their definitions in every condition. Level~4
withholds all children of each multi-child parent group and again evaluates the
complete candidate set. The description-complete path
\texttt{L1-D $\rightarrow$ L2-D $\rightarrow$ L3-DD $\rightarrow$ L4-D}
forms the primary difficulty curve. Compound organisation and taxonomy shift is
retained as deferred future work rather than included in the active scope.

The raw F1 values at different levels are not treated as interchangeable
benchmarks. The important evidence is the within-method degradation along the
registered path, together with matched within-level interventions.

\section{Model and Information Controls}

Five model families span increasing representational and adaptation capacity:
strict train-only TF--IDF, frozen E5-base-v2 embeddings, a supervised
DistilBERT review--candidate cross-encoder, frozen candidate-pair Qwen, and
candidate-pair QLoRA. Every method receives the same conceptual claim:
\emph{review, candidate aspect representation, candidate sentiment}. Count
bag-of-words remains a Level~1 sanity control rather than a core cross-level
method.

The aspect representation is explicitly controlled. A name-only condition
contains the full canonical hierarchical name. A description condition adds
one short, frozen, neutral taxonomy gloss written from the names, hierarchy,
and general language knowledge only. No review text, corpus keyword
statistics, validation or test labels, predictions, or error analysis may be
used to construct or revise these descriptions. Consequently, the treatment is
described as \emph{description-assisted zero-shot generalisation}, not
zero-information classification.

\section{Evaluation Commitments}

All active methods preserve aspect-conditioned sentiment, permit different
sentiments for different aspects in one review, and produce a set of
aspect--sentiment pairs. Hyperparameters, thresholds, prompts, and checkpoints
are selected from seen-aspect validation evidence only within each outer
training scope. Held-out-aspect validation labels cannot calibrate the strict
difficulty curve, and the test set is used only after the configuration is
frozen.

The analysis separates overall, seen, and unseen performance. It reports pair
micro- and macro-F1, corrected positive-class presence precision, recall and
F1, exact match, false-positive and false-negative rows, prediction-set size,
and conditional sentiment diagnostics. Point estimates are accompanied by
paired review-cluster confidence intervals for the registered primary
comparisons. Per-aspect or per-group differences are shown separately so that
review-sampling uncertainty is not confused with variation across taxonomic
units or training seeds.

\section{Scope of the Contribution}

The dissertation does not claim unrestricted topic discovery, universal
zero-shot learning, or that a larger model must outperform a smaller one. Its
contribution is a controlled and auditable study of candidate-label
aspect-sentiment classification under increasingly difficult taxonomy change.
The central outputs are the difficulty curve, the dual-unseen asymmetric
description crossover, and the matched frozen-to-QLoRA adaptation comparison.
Earlier closed-taxonomy, held-out-organisation, hosted Gemini, and router
experiments provide project provenance or secondary context, but they do not
define the active thesis mainline.
